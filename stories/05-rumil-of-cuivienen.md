# Rúmil of Cuiviénen

*Before the Sun and Moon. The story of the blind Elf from Story 3, told from the very beginning.*

## Chapter one: under the stars

Rúmil was born by the waters of Cuiviénen, when the Elves had only just woken. His sister Elenwë was born a few years after him. She was the singer, always naming things. He was the hunter, always wanting to see past the next hill. Even then, Elves who wandered too far vanished, and the elders spoke of a Dark Rider in the woods.

When Rúmil was young his friend Tarvo went out for berries and never came back. Rúmil found the basket overturned beside huge hoof-prints. The elders forbade anyone to follow.

> *The player chose: he goes to find his friend.*

Elenwë caught him leaving and gave him a small carved bird she'd made. He followed the tracks three nights north to a ravine. Below, in a cage of black thorns, were three Elves, Tarvo among them, and beside it a huge dark shape on a black horse.

Then a hand on his arm: Elenwë had followed him the whole way without a sound.

> *The player chose: he tells her to stay there.*

He climbed down. The thorns were cold as ice and sharp as blades. The horse stamped, and the rider's hood began to turn.

> *The player chose: he isn't scared. He's brave.*

Rúmil stepped out with an arrow nocked. "Let them go." The rider laughed like cracking ice. His arrow vanished into the hood. He rolled from the hooves and shot the horse, and it reared.

> *The player's direction: the early Elves had little need of weapons. What they had was a young magic, song, and fearless hearts.*

Rúmil let his bow fall and joined the song Elenwë was already singing from the top of the ravine: the song of the first waking, when they opened their eyes and saw the stars. The thorns withered. Tarvo and the others came out singing too. Five voices. The rider howled and fled north.

> *The player chose: they don't follow. They go home with the news, and the song of hope that keeps the dark away.*

From then on, every night at Cuiviénen, someone kept watch with a song instead of a bow. And the Elves stopped disappearing.

## Oromë

Far away, Oromë heard voices singing in a language no one had taught them, and turned his horse towards them.

> *The player's direction: they were afraid at first, because of the Black Rider.*

Panic tore through the camp. Some fled into the hills for good. But Rúmil and Elenwë stood and sang, and the rider climbed down and sang with them, with a voice like wind through a whole forest. The wise ones knew him for kin of the Valar, because his song was of the colours in the sky: ribbons of green, violet and pale gold rippling over the stars, like the northern lights.

Oromë told them of the Valar and of Valinor and the light of the Two Trees, and asked them to come West.

> *The player chose: Rúmil stays. He wants to go north and find the Elves who went missing.*

"You'll take the ones who are here to safety. Who'll go looking for the ones who aren't?" Oromë said that was a hunter's answer, and gave him a small horn carved from antler. "If you're ever in the dark with no song left, blow this."

> *The player chose: Elenwë goes, for now. And she foretells they'll meet again.*

At the parting by the water, her eyes went distant. "This isn't the end. We'll stand together again, under these same stars, before the world changes." He pressed the carved bird back into her hand: "Then bring this back to me. That's a promise now." He listened to her singing until the host was gone. A few hundred remained: the ones who would be called the Avari.

## Ulmo

Rúmil went north alone for many years, through darker and colder forests. He buried the bones of things that weren't quite Elves any more and sang over them. He was lonely, and sometimes thought of following the host.

> *The player's direction: Ulmo hears of the Avari and won't forsake them. Lore and skill pass to Rúmil through the waters.*

One night, with his feet in a cold mountain river, the water spoke. "The others have forgotten the ones who stayed. I haven't. Wherever there's water, I'll hear you." Rúmil woke knowing how to read a river's mood, find hidden springs, tell clean water from poisoned, and hear, with his hands in a stream, what had passed upstream. That morning he heard Elves weeping far to the north.

## Chapter two: the river-folk

The first holding-pit was a ring of stakes between two hills, with fires, chained wolves, a dozen young orcs and one tall hooded shape that never slept. Rúmil asked the river for a fog, and it rose.

> *The player's direction: he doesn't just sing the old song. He sings fear and confusion and wishing to be elsewhere, then turns it into a chorus of unlocking and freeing.*

The song of fear made the orcs shiver. The song of confusion turned the camp the wrong way round. The song of longing sent them drifting off into the mist, back towards the safe dark tunnels. Even the hooded shape forgot what it guarded. Then the song of unlocking: knots slipping loose, iron forgetting how to hold, doors remembering they were made to open. Ropes fell, chains clicked open, stakes leaned apart. Thirty captives walked into the river, which washed their footprints away.

A young Elf, Lindo, said twenty more had been taken north a week before.

> *The player chose: they'll rescue every one they can. And every night on the road, Rúmil tells the story of Oromë and the Valar.*

Most had been taken before Oromë came and had never heard any of it. Lindo asked for the same stories again and again. An older Elf, Nessa, asked, "If the West is so wonderful, why did you stay?" "Because you were here." After that they called themselves the people of the river, who sang in the dark.

## The cave of the false song

> *The player chose: nobody is guarding the twenty.*

They sat in a cave mouth, unchained, staring inwards. From deep inside came a voice, beautiful, singing of rest, of how pointless it was to run. A song like Rúmil's, turned inside out. Melkor's servants had learned from the singing in the dark. Rúmil's feet grew heavy. Nessa sat down on a rock. Lindo's eyes glazed.

> *The player chose: he blows the horn.*

The call of the Great Hunt roared through every chamber. Things fled below with claws scraping. The twenty woke. Then the whole river-folk sang a song of resisting, feet planted and eyes open, then of freedom, wind and rivers and stars no dark can put out. Thirty-eight walked away together.

That night a small crack had appeared in the horn.

> *The player's direction: with his ear to it, he hears running water. Ulmo is guiding him again.*

The sound was louder facing west, fainter facing north. "Not north. Not any more." Some were angry. Nessa, who'd nearly been lost in that cave, said, "That thing learned our song. Next time it'll learn the horn. If the Lord of Waters says west, I'll go west."

## The dwarves

The horn led them to a gorge where a river vanished into a dark arch in the mountainside. Beside it, carvings of straight lines and sharp angles.

> *The player chose: it's clearly a language, not the shadow's. Look for more.*

Waymarks pointing along the gorge. A small, beautifully made hammer in the shallows. Short, broad, heavy-booted footprints into the arch. And through the water, a steady sound from deep in the mountain: hammering.

> *The player chose: the camp waits; Rúmil and his closest go in.*

Rúmil, Nessa and Lindo waded through the dark to a square chamber lit red by a forge, where a Dwarf, broad as a doorway, beard tucked into his belt, was beating out a glowing bar and singing deep and rough.

> *The player's direction, retold: they weren't afraid. They were amazed. The first other living thing that sang. Lindo sang to complement the Dwarf's work, of things stable and strong.*

Lindo wove his song around the rhythm of the hammer: roots gripping the mountain, stone holding the world together, iron that doesn't bend. Nessa joined, then Rúmil. Where the song touched the metal it gleamed with a silver shimmer. The Dwarf turned slowly, eyes wet. "Again."

He was **Frár**. He thumped his chest and said it, got their names slightly wrong, and laughed a huge booming laugh. The hammer from the river was his.

> *The player's direction: beautiful halls. This is where these Dwarves woke, and they started work straight away. The Elves stay a long time, bring the whole camp, learn the Dwarvish tongue. The Dwarves teach the Elves the deep names, so the Elves can sing runes into what's made.*

Frár led them to a vast hall of stone pillars carved like trees, channels of water catching the firelight, runes on every wall. Frár banged his hammer on an anvil until everyone stopped, and the Elves sang, and the hall fell silent. The river-folk came up from the gorge. In time the Dwarves gave them the true names of iron, granite and silver, and the Elves sang them into blades that stayed keen, beams that never cracked, locks only their makers could open. For a long time they were happy.

## The song of Oromë

> *The player's direction: the Elves tell their whole story. The Dwarves have heard rumours of shadows. The Elves sing the song of Oromë into Frár's armour and axe.*

At a winter feast Rúmil told it all, from the waking to the cave of the false song. The eldest Dwarf said shadows had whispered at the edges of their halls too, promising gems, warning the rock above was rotten. Miners had followed and never returned. Now they had a name for it.

"This Oromë," said Frár. "I'd like to carry some of him with me."

So the Elves sang the song of Oromë into his armour and axe: the great hunt, hooves like thunder, the horn that scattered Melkor's creatures, the colours in the sky. The Dwarves rumbled the deep names of iron and steel in time. Frár's axe sang through the air like a distant horn, and his mail rippled green and violet like the northern lights.

## The first jealousy

> *The player's direction: the armour splits the Dwarves. Some are jealous of its power. The first murmurings against the Elves.*

Durlin had been the finest smith in the hall. Now everyone wanted what Frár had, and no hammering of Durlin's could make anything shine like that. He said, quietly, over his forge, that the deep names belonged to the Dwarves and should never have been given away. That the Elves took more than they gave. And then: that if the Elves could sing such power into one Dwarf's armour, they should never be allowed to leave and take it to anyone else.

Some Dwarves began to nod.

## Chapter three: the road west

### The voice in the water

The thaw came late that year. The forges burned day and night, because every Dwarf who'd seen Frár's armour wanted something sung into their own work, and the Elves sang until they were hoarse. But the hall was cooling towards them in small ways. The Dwarves stopped thumping the benches after a song. A door in the lower mines that Lindo had used a hundred times was locked, and nobody could say why. Nessa started sitting where she could see the great doors.

One evening Rúmil sat by the water channel that ran past Durlin's forge with his fingers in the current, and the water brought him voices.

> *The player asked: what did he hear?*

Durlin was talking low with old Thrandur, keeper of the great doors. The Elves would want to leave with the spring meltwater, Durlin said, so that was when they'd act. The arch could be sealed with a single stone, and once it was down it was down for good. "They can sing all they like to a mountain." Thrandur asked about Frár, who would surely go with them. The hammering stopped. "Then Frár stays too. One way or another."

### The escape

> *The player chose: Rúmil tells the group in secret and confides in Frár. They plan to escape by night without unrest. Frár insists no Dwarf is hurt, and won't harm one of his own, however upset he is.*

Rúmil told Nessa and Lindo on the upper galleries, where the waterfalls drowned every voice. Lindo went white. Nessa only nodded, as if she'd been waiting for it. Then they told Frár at his forge. He was quiet a long time. "Durlin taught me to hold a hammer. I'll go with you. But not one Dwarf is to be hurt. Not even him."

Word went round the river-folk in whispers in the Elvish tongue, which few Dwarves had bothered to learn. Packs were filled a little at a time. On the night of the first great meltwater, when the river was too loud for anyone to hear footsteps, they went down the long stair to the arch, Frár first with his shimmering mail hidden under his cloak.

At the foot of the stair a lantern was burning. Thrandur stood alone by the sealing stone with his hand on the lever.

> *The player left it to the storyteller.*

Thrandur had waited three nights for them. Durlin said the Elves took more than they gave, he said, but he'd been there when Lindo sang the cracks out of the east pillar, a pillar he'd been patching for forty years. He took his hand off the lever. "Go, before I think better of it. I'll tell Durlin the river was too loud and I fell asleep at my post. It won't be the first time he's believed that of me."

The river-folk waded out through the arch into the starlight, and five more Dwarves who'd chosen to follow Frár came with them. Long after they were safely through, Rúmil heard the slow grinding of the stone as the arch sealed behind them.

### The girdle of Melian

> *The player's direction: the water leads them through a pass in the mountains and down towards Beleriand, into the Girdle of Melian.*

They walked until the stars paled over the western mountains. Rúmil put the cracked horn to his ear, and the water said west. For weeks they climbed a pass so narrow the Dwarves had to turn sideways, and every night the horn said the same thing. Then they came over the last ridge and saw a wide green land of rivers and forests running off the edge of sight. "That's not a land," said Frár. "That's a whole world."

They came down into great woods of beech and oak, and the woods would not let them in. Paths turned back on themselves. They found their own footprints from the morning. A mist rose that wasn't the river's, and when Rúmil asked the water to clear it, for the first time since Ulmo spoke to him the water didn't answer. Nessa touched a tree and pulled her hand back. "There's a song in this wood, older and stronger than anything we know." Lindo sat down in the moss. "So we've come all this way to be turned away at the door?"

> *The player's direction: they sing a song of searching and finding, of longing and friendship. The wood sends visions. The Elves see the Dark Rider, then Oromë, and know the wood is friendly. The Dwarves are afraid, and Rúmil helps them understand. He sings the story of the escape, and how the Dwarves helped them.*

Rúmil sang something new, a song of searching and finding, of longing for someone far away, and of friends who walk beside you when the road is long. The river-folk joined him, and the mist took shape. Out of it came the Black Rider from the ravine, hood turning towards them. Lindo cried out. But they kept singing, and the black horse turned white, the hood fell back, and there was Oromë with the northern lights behind him. The wood was showing them their own hearts, the fear first and the hope beneath it.

The Dwarves saw their own visions: the lost miners who had followed the whispers, pale and silent among the trees. A young Dwarf, Nali, turned to run, and Rúmil caught his arm. "It's not a trap. It's a question. The wood is asking who we are." Then he sang the escape from the hall: Frár choosing friendship over his own people, Thrandur lifting his hand from the lever, the Dwarves walking out into the starlight beside the Elves. Frár rumbled along in the deep tongue, then the others. The pale miners bowed their heads and faded, and the mist parted on an open path.

On it stood a grey-cloaked Elf with a lantern.

> *The player's direction: a guard from Menegroth. He welcomes them, but he's never seen a Dwarf, so he's amazed and wary. There are rumours of something in the hills, but he knows no more than that.*

He was one of Beleg's march-wardens, sent by the Queen to meet whoever was singing at her border. "You sang the wood open. Few can." Then his lantern fell on Frár, and he stepped back with his hand going to his bow. There was talk in the eastern marches, he said, of short strong folk hammering in the deep places. He'd taken it for a tale to frighten the young. "Frár, son of nobody you'll have heard of," said the Dwarf. "And I'm no tale, lad." Rúmil told the warden they were friends who'd chosen the Elves over their own hall. The warden lowered his hand and bowed, a little stiffly. "Then you're welcome under the trees, Master Frár. Though the King's people will stare."

### Menegroth

He led them along paths that opened just ahead and closed just behind, over a stone bridge across a dark, fast river, to the doors of Menegroth, the Thousand Caves.

> *The player's direction: more Elves and more civilisation than Rúmil thought possible. Beautiful works of art, people singing in groups, deeper and larger than anything he'd imagined.*

The hall beyond was so vast its far end was lost in golden light. Pillars carved as beech trees, silver lanterns in their stone branches, carved birds and real birds singing back at them, fountains in white marble, tapestries of stories Rúmil didn't know, among them a king and a lady meeting in a wood. And Elves everywhere, more than had ever lived at Cuiviénen, while somewhere deep in the caves a choir sang in harmonies that made his own songs feel like a child's humming.

Lindo wept. Nessa whispered, "This is what they made, while we were hiding in the dark." Frár ran his fingers over a carved pillar. "Whoever cut this knew the stone's true name." Rúmil couldn't speak. He'd spent his life thinking the river-folk were most of what was left of his people.

Then one voice rose alone above the choir, and he knew it.

> *The player chose: he turns, laughs, and runs to his sister.*

His laugh turned every head in the hall. He ran past the fountains and down into a lower cave where the choir stood in a ring of lantern light, and the voices dropped away until only hers was left, and then that stopped too. Elenwë was in grey and pale blue like the Queen's maidens, silver in her hair, older and wiser, with exactly the same eyes. They met halfway across the floor and held on until neither could breathe.

She drew out the carved bird, worn smooth with years of holding. "I told you. Under the same stars, before the world changes." He said she hadn't gone West. "No." She glanced up at a tall woman in white watching from the stair, her eyes full of an old, deep light. "I found someone here who needed me more. And I knew if I waited, you'd come."

She pressed the bird into his hand and closed his fingers around it.

### Thingol and Melian's warning

> *The player's direction: this is early Menegroth, before the Dwarves of Belegost came, just the beginnings of the halls. Frár and his friends are the first Dwarves to help build it, and will later go back to their kin in Belegost and tell them of Queen Melian. Thingol is glad to receive them, and the Dwarves are eager to give back with the axe and the tools the Elves helped them make. But when they tell of the jealousy in their hall, Melian foresees it will happen again. She doesn't know what a Silmaril is, but she knows something like it is coming.*

Thingol received them in a cave that was still half rough stone, with torches where silver lanterns would one day hang. When he saw Frár he laughed with delight, and asked straight away whether stone like this could be shaped into a hall worthy of a king. Frár ran his hand along the rock and grinned. "Give us time, and tools, and a few of your singers, and it'll be the wonder of the world." The Dwarves set to work that season, cutting with the axe that sang like a horn and the tools the Elves had sung the deep names into. It was their way, they said, of giving back.

At the feast that night Frár told the King and Queen the whole tale: Durlin's jealousy, the sealed arch, old Thrandur at the lever. Thingol frowned. Melian went very still, and her eyes grew distant the way Elenwë's once had by the water at Cuiviénen.

"Jealousy over a made thing," she said softly. "It came once, and it will come again. One day a treasure will be brought into these halls, brighter than anything you've made, Master Frár. Dwarves and Elves will both desire it, and that desire will cost this kingdom dearly." For a moment there was sorrow in her face as she looked at Thingol. Then it passed. "But not tonight."

Frár swore that he and his would only ever bring good to this house, and that when the first halls were finished he'd go west to the mountains, find his kin in Belegost and tell them of the Queen who saw the future.

> *The player's direction: nobody can really make sense of her words, so nobody takes much note, and they carry on.*

A treasure brighter than anything Frár could make? There was no such thing in all the world. Thingol called for more wine, the harpers began again, and by morning the Queen's words had faded like a dream half remembered.

So the work went on. Season after season the Dwarves cut deeper into the hill, and the Elves sang the stone into beech trees and birds. Rúmil walked the woods with Elenwë, and they told each other everything that had happened in the long years apart. For a while, all was quiet in Doriath.

> *The player chose: that's the end of this part of the story.*

## Where we left off

This part ends quietly in Doriath. Rúmil and Elenwë are together again, the river-folk have settled, and Frár and his five Dwarves are delving the first halls of Menegroth, with Frár planning to go to Belegost when they're done.
